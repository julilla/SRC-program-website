What should every Brown CS graduate know about computing’s impact on society?

The Integrated Curriculum for Socially Responsible Computing is our answer. It sets out the social, ethical, and political knowledge and skills we want students to build over the course of a CS degree, and suggests where in the curriculum each topic fits best. Rather than gathering these questions into a single ethics course, it weaves them into technical courses, from introductory sequences to upper-level electives, so that students meet them where they actually arise in computing work and deepen their understanding as they progress.

The curriculum is a resource for instructors and teaching staff, including our Socially Responsible Computing Teaching Assistants (STAs), deciding what SRC content to include in a course, and for students and colleagues who want to see how responsible computing fits into a computer science education. It is organized into five topic areas. Each one contains learning objectives that state what students should be able to do, at which skill level, and in which kinds of courses we suggest the content be taught.

Five Skill Levels

Each learning objective names the skill level students should reach. The levels build on one another, from knowing that an issue exists to acting on it in technical work. Throughout this page, they are marked with these colored labels.

  1. 1

    Be aware of

    Students have heard of a concept/idea and how it relates to a given (technical) context. Students are able to recall the concept/idea as relevant in a specific context. (“I know X is a thing.”)

  2. 2

    Understand

    Students can explain the meaning and/or significance of a specific concept/idea. They can articulate how it functions and/or why it is important.

  3. 3

    Identify

    Students are able to recognize relevant concerns in a specific technical context without prompting and are able to articulate how they would affect relevant technical decisions.

  4. 4

    Analyze

    Students are able to critically evaluate a specific concept/idea, either abstractly or in a specific (social or technical) context. They are able to cite and engage with different concerns and/or (conflicting) perspectives on the topic in question.

  5. 5

    Implement

    Students are able to execute concrete technical decisions or use specific techniques to achieve a desired objective.

I.a.1: Representing aspects of the world through data is necessarily reductive and incomplete. Developer perspective shapes the choice and design of variables.

  1. Be aware of

    Students have heard of the limitations of data and developer perspective for creating truly representative models. (“I know all models are imperfect.”)

    For example: They have heard of the impossibility of creating a comprehensive climate model.

  2. Understand

    Students can explain the meaning and/or significance of representing aspects of the world through data. They can articulate how data and datasets lead to imperfect models, how developer choice will shape the design of the model, and why data are important to interpreting and building incomplete and reductive models.

    For example: Students can explain why no dataset can possibly cover all relevant variables when building a model predicting the rise of temperatures in Latin America, and how/why the choices of what to include/exclude will impact any model, including a climate model.

  3. Identify

    Students are able to recognize the limitations of a given model or dataset without prompting. They are able to articulate how the choice and design of variables affect a model.

    For example: Given a dataset of historical temperatures in Latin American countries, students are able to recognize the inclusion/exclusion of some country or variable, such as cloud cover, wind speed, or time of temperature recorded will impact the accuracy of a climate model.

  4. Analyze

    Students are able to critically evaluate choice and design of variables in creating an imperfect model. They are able to cite and engage with different concerns and/or (conflicting) perspectives on creating models.

    For example: Students are able to critically evaluate whether data on rising sea levels is or is not important to include in a climate model, and how this potentially interacts with other values, such as runtime, transparency, or accessibility.

  5. Implement

    Students are able to execute concrete technical decisions or use specific techniques to achieve a desired model for their purposes and within the appropriate context.

    For example: Students are able to execute feature engineering or hyperparameter tuning to further the accuracy of their model, while recognizing that their model will still be necessarily reductive and incomplete.
